Introduction

Whether waist circumference is associated with HbA1c beyond established risk factors is uncertain in nationally representative data. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data permit design-based estimation that generalizes to the noninstitutionalized US population. We aimed to evaluate whether waist circumference is associated with HbA1c among US adults.

Methods

Data Source

NHANES is a continuous, nationally representative survey of the US civilian noninstitutionalized population using a stratified, multistage probability design, with household interviews and standardized physical examinations and laboratory testing in mobile examination centers (MECs). We used NHANES 2017-March 2020 pre-pandemic. NHANES protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board and participants provided written informed consent; this analysis of deidentified public-use data did not require additional institutional review board review. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous outcome: survey-weighted linear regression (design-based Taylor-series SEs) estimates adjusted mean differences. A binary clinical threshold of the outcome is analysed as a sensitivity analysis. All analyses incorporated the WTMECPRP sample weight (mec component), masked variance pseudo-strata and pseudo-PSUs (Taylor series linearization; 24 strata, 49 PSUs, design df = 25); subpopulations were analyzed with domain (subset) estimation to preserve the full design. Proportions use Korn-Graubard 95% CIs and were evaluated against the NCHS Data Presentation Standards for Proportions. Regression inference used t distributions with design degrees of freedom. Covariates were selected as plausible common causes of waist circumference and HbA1c (minimal confounder set from the domain library): Age, Sex, Race and ethnicity, Income-to-poverty ratio, Educational attainment, Smoking status, and Body mass index. Body mass index: BMI adjustment isolates the contribution of central adiposity beyond overall weight ('at similar weight'). Expect collinearity with waist (VIF check). Variables on the causal pathway or sharing laboratory components with the outcome/exposure are excluded to avoid over-adjustment. Effect modification was assessed with Wald F tests of product terms (Sex, Age group). Complete-case primary analysis within the design; multiple imputation by chained equations as sensitivity analysis when covariates are missing. Prespecified sensitivity analyses: Binary outcome: Elevated HbA1c (≥6.5%) (PR); Exclude participants on glucose-lowering therapy; Exclude anemia (HbA1c unreliable); Multiple imputation of missing covariates (m = 10); Exposure in weighted quartiles (Q4 vs Q1, P for trend); Alternative exposure: Abdominal obesity - waist >102 cm (men) / >88 cm (women); Alternative exposure: Central adiposity - waist-to-height ratio ≥0.5; Unweighted analysis (design ignored). E-values quantified the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding needed to explain away the primary association. Two-sided P < .05 denoted significance. Analyses used R version 4.6.1 (2026-06-24) with survey 4.5, mice 3.19.0 and ggplot2 4.0.3, orchestrated by Q2E.

Results

The analytic sample included 7,988 participants (weighted to represent approximately 226.3 million US residents; 51.1% were female (weighted) and 20.1% were aged 65 years or older). Overall, waist circumference per 10 cm was associated with HbA1c (β, 0.15 (95% CI, 0.10-0.21)) after adjustment for age, sex, race and ethnicity, income-to-poverty ratio, educational attainment, smoking status, body mass index. 7 of 8 sensitivity analyses agree in direction (associations) or magnitude (prevalence). Divergent: Alternative exposure: Central adiposity - waist-to-height ratio ≥0.5

The E-value for the primary estimate was 1.52 (CI limit 1.38); Mean difference converted via standardized effect (RR ~ exp(0.91 d)).

Effect modification by sex: P = .31 for interaction (Male: 0.13 (0.02-0.24); Female: 0.17 (0.11-0.23)).

Effect modification by age group: P < .001 for interaction (18-39: 0.04 (-0.05-0.12); 40-64: 0.18 (0.11-0.26); 65+: 0.25 (0.13-0.38)).

Discussion

In this nationally representative cross-sectional analysis, waist circumference per 10 cm was associated with HbA1c (β, 0.15 (95% CI, 0.10-0.21)) after adjustment for age, sex, race and ethnicity, income-to-poverty ratio, educational attainment, smoking status, body mass index. Strengths include a prespecified, investigator-approved protocol; guideline-versioned, citable case definitions; design-based variance estimation with appropriate examination weights; NCHS reliability standards; multiple prespecified sensitivity analyses; and a fully reproducible, provenance-tracked pipeline. Limitations: Cross-sectional design: temporality cannot be established and associations must not be interpreted causally. Single measurement of laboratory values; within-person variability and chronicity cannot be assessed. Residual confounding by unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors (e.g., diet, medications, menstrual blood loss). Self-reported comorbidities and behaviours are subject to recall and social-desirability bias. NHANES excludes institutionalized and active-duty military populations.

Limitations

  • Cross-sectional design: temporality cannot be established and associations must not be interpreted causally.
  • Single measurement of laboratory values; within-person variability and chronicity cannot be assessed.
  • Residual confounding by unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors (e.g., diet, medications, menstrual blood loss).
  • Self-reported comorbidities and behaviours are subject to recall and social-desirability bias.
  • NHANES excludes institutionalized and active-duty military populations.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of US adults, waist circumference per 10 cm was associated with HbA1c (β, 0.15 (95% CI, 0.10-0.21)) after adjustment for age, sex, race and ethnicity, income-to-poverty ratio, educational attainment, smoking status, body mass index. This association is cross-sectional and may reflect residual confounding or reverse causation; prospective studies are needed to establish temporality.
